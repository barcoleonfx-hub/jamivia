The gut

The gut lining, explained in five minutes

A single layer of cells, folded into millions of tiny fingers, sits between everything you eat and the rest of your body.

4 min read · Jamivia Journal

Most of us picture the gut as a tube. Up close, its inner surface looks more like a landscape: a soft, folded floor covered in millions of tiny projections called villi, each one lined with cells that are themselves covered in even smaller folds.

One cell thick

The lining of the small intestine is a single layer of cells, the epithelium. It has two jobs that pull in opposite directions. It has to let nutrients through, and it has to keep almost everything else out.

The cells manage this together. Where neighbouring cells meet, protein structures called tight junctions seal the gaps between them, deciding what can pass along the edges of the cells rather than through them.

Folded into villi and microvilli, the small intestine's inner surface is estimated at around 30 square metres, roughly the floor of a small flat.

Built to be replaced

This is one of the fastest-renewing tissues in the body. New cells are made at the base of each villus and travel up towards its tip over a few days, where they are shed and replaced. The whole surface is rebuilt continuously, every week of your life.

The layer on top

Above the cells sits a layer of mucus. In the large intestine it has two parts: a dense inner layer that is largely kept clear of bacteria, and a looser outer layer where many of the gut's microbes live. Below the lining, the gut holds a large share of the body's immune tissue.
